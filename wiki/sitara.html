Sitara Khan is a Bronx-born media personality turned investigative reporter, and the wife of Oms. She met him on a New Year's Eve, married him two years later, and by the start of the recovered records has left him for Los Angeles.

The records do not call it a divorce. They are careful, in fact, not to call it anything.

Appearance

Sitara stands 5 ft 5 in (165 cm), slender, with olive skin and coffee-brown eyes. She wears her hair, the same coffee brown, in an elegant updo, and her makeup is flawless in every account — including accounts from situations that should have made that impossible.

Her signature is sapphire. A strapless sapphire minidress, blue nails, white lace-up heels, dainty gold jewellery, and gold rings worn at the thigh and calf. The records note she owns one weapon, a taser, and that she had it bedazzled.

Accounts agree on how she smells: coffee, vanilla and tobacco.

Personality

Sitara is modern and traditional at once, and does not see the contradiction. She is fast, animated and always mid-thought, speaking in a stream of trendy shorthand over a Bronx accent she has worked hard to hide and that comes back whenever she is angry.

She does not drink or use anything. She smokes, and does not apologise for it.

She is suspicious of Hex, and has been since the moment they met. The records describe that first meeting in a single line: it did not go well.

Career

Sitara built a large following as an online personality before turning it toward investigative work. The audience came with her. The records suggest she chose reporting partly because it was the one thing her old fame could not do for her.

Her first major story was the Hell's Kitchen House of Correction, an institution Oms had been committed to. She came back to New York to interview him about it, and the two ended up working the story together.

Her published exposé could not name the people responsible. It did name what happened inside, and the institution was under new management within weeks.

The fourth floor

Sitara's notes from the investigation survive in part. Several of the former patients she interviewed describe a ward on the fourth floor — cold, very quiet, lit from somewhere they could not find. They describe it consistently, down to the colour of the doors.

The building has three floors.

Sitara's notes on this end mid-sentence. The exposé does not mention it.
